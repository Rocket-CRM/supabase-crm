# Sales Presentation Slide Principles

## Purpose

These principles guide Rocket sales presentation slides for B2B SaaS buyers, especially marketing teams evaluating Loyalty CRM and loyalty program platforms.

The goal is not a prettier feature tour. The goal is a buyer-ready decision story: clear in the meeting, structured enough for the champion to forward internally, and specific enough that marketing, finance, IT, and leadership can understand why Rocket is the right system.

## Core Narrative

Use this as the default Rocket loyalty sales story:

1. Customer expectations changed: customers expect brands to know them, reward them, and personalize the relationship.
2. Most loyalty programs stop too early: points and rewards create members, but not necessarily active customers.
3. Marketing teams need a system that moves through three stages:
   - Convert: turn customers into known members and collect first-party data.
   - Engage: give members reasons to interact beyond purchase.
   - Activate: use data and AI to take the right action for each customer.
4. Rocket is the Loyalty CRM that connects those stages in one customer profile and one action system.

## Slide Structure

### 1. Lead With The Buyer, Not Rocket

The customer is the hero. Rocket is the guide and system.

Bad:

> Rocket is an AI-powered Loyalty CRM with comprehensive features.

Better:

> Your marketing team can turn every purchase into data, every member into an engaged customer, and every customer signal into an automated next action.

### 2. Use The Right Category Altitude

For marketing teams, default to `Loyalty CRM`.

Use:

- `Loyalty CRM` for marketing leaders evaluating tools.
- `Loyalty program` or `ระบบสะสมแต้ม` for practical/search-friendly contexts.
- `Agentic B2C CRM` for vision, leadership, PR, investor, and brand-level moments.

Do not lead cold buyers with high-altitude language before they understand what the product is.

### 3. Follow A Decision Story

A sales deck should mirror how a B2B buyer decides:

1. Market shift
2. Current problem
3. Cost of doing nothing
4. Better approach
5. Rocket solution
6. How it works
7. Proof
8. Implementation path
9. Risks / objections
10. Specific next step

For shorter decks, compress this into:

> Problem → Cost → Solution → Proof → Plan → Next Step

### 4. Choose The Headline Type By Slide Role

Slide titles should make the slide immediately scannable. Do not force every headline into a conclusion or action-title format. Product and marketing buyers often prefer short evidence labels, feature labels, or journey labels when those are clearer than a sentence.

Use the headline style that matches the slide role:

- Proof / traction slides: preserve the evidence in a short label. Do not invent a broad market conclusion from a screenshot, event photo, or report image.
- Feature-library slides: use the feature name or product category when that is clearest.
- Journey / demo slides: use a concise journey label, not an abstract operating principle.
- Section slides: name the category and buyer problem.
- Company slides: position Rocket plainly; avoid PR phrases.

Clarity beats cleverness. If a headline names only an outcome, metric, or benefit but hides what the feature actually is, add the feature category in plain language or use the feature category as the headline. A buyer should know what they are looking at within five seconds.

For feature-library slides, the feature name itself is often the clearest headline. High-intent buyers already know they are checking whether Rocket has `Rewards`, `Tiers`, `Missions`, `Referrals`, `Dashboard`, or `Workflow`. Do not force a "sexy" benefit headline if it becomes abstract. Use the feature name as the header, then make the body prove scope, behavior, and buyer value.

Weak:

> Omnichannel Loyalty

Too abstract:

> Every purchase channel should build the same customer profile.

Stronger:

> Omnichannel Point Collection

Also acceptable:

> Purchase to Profile to Reward

Weak proof headline:

> Rocket brings the Loyalty CRM conversation to the market.

Stronger proof headlines:

> Leading innovation in Thailand

> Most likely to choose CRM in 2025

> Top B2C CRM consideration set

Weak:

> Increase ROI by 30% with always-on automation.

Stronger:

> Customer Service AI - Chat

Also acceptable:

> Customer Service AI - Chat resolves common questions before they reach an agent.

Also acceptable for feature-library slides:

> Rewards

Then the body must answer what is configurable, what the customer sees, and what the operator controls.

Weak:

> AI Personalization

Strong:

> AI turns customer data into the next best action automatically.

### 5. One Main Message Per Slide

Each slide needs one governing thought. Supporting text, diagrams, screenshots, and numbers must all support that thought.

If a slide has two conclusions, split it.

Write the slide as one composed artifact, not as independent text fields. Before changing copy, read the title, body, labels, diagram, screenshot, and callouts together. Decide what the visual already proves, then make the text add the missing business meaning.

Rules:

- The headline should frame the slide's concept or decision point.
- Body copy should explain why the visual matters, not narrate what the audience can already see.
- Labels should help scanning; they should not become a second paragraph broken into fragments.
- Do not fill feature slides with every possible product keyword. Choose the altitude the slide needs: concept, model, workflow, feature scope, or implementation detail.

Examples:

- A campaign → Customer 360 → activation timeline should not be titled with a narrow tactic like "Campaigns should trigger the next best action." If the visual is showing the ideal customer loop, use the concept: `Perfect retention journey`.
- A rule-engine diagram should not dump every possible action, condition, and outcome. Explain the concept at the right altitude: `Trigger → Decision → Result`.

### 6. Translate Thai Slides For Understanding, Not Literal Purity

When writing Thai slide content, keep technical product terms in English when there is no natural Thai equivalent or when the English term is the market-standard phrase.

Keep terms like:

- `Omnichannel`
- `Customer Service AI`
- `Chat`
- `CRM`
- `Dashboard`
- `Workflow`
- `Segment`
- `Campaign`

Translate the surrounding explanation into natural Thai, but do not force awkward Thai replacements for technical words. Define the term once if needed, then use it consistently.

## Applying Core Structure To Slides

### 7. Use Core Structure At Deck, Section, And Slide Level

Apply `CORE_WRITING_PRINCIPLES.md` to every deck:

- Pyramid structure: the deck, section, and slide each need a core concept.
- Vertical logic: every supporting point must serve the slide's main message.
- Horizontal logic: peer items should be grouped by one dimension.
- MECE: sections and slide groups should avoid overlaps and obvious gaps.
- Same-level abstraction: flat lists should not mix category, feature, channel, outcome, and UI detail.

Good Rocket section grouping:

> Convert → Engage → Activate

Each stage has a clear role:

- Convert: registration, points, unified profile, omnichannel collection.
- Engage: tiers, rewards, missions, referrals, gamified campaigns.
- Activate: segmentation, churn prediction, personalized offers, automated campaigns.

### 8. Same-Level Discipline Applies To Peer Items On Slides

Do not mix abstraction levels inside a flat slide list.

Bad:

> Loyalty CRM / Points / AI / LINE / Increase retention / Dashboard

This mixes category, feature, technology, channel, outcome, and interface.

Good, grouped by capability:

> Points / Tiers / Rewards / Missions / Referrals

Good, grouped by outcome:

> Convert customers / Engage members / Activate data

Good, grouped by channel:

> POS / Online Store / Marketplace / Receipt Upload / QR Scan

### 9. Visual Structures May Mix Levels If The Relationship Is Explicit

Overview, journey, and system-flow slides can mix levels because their job is to show how things connect.

Allowed:

- Overview map: `Convert → Engage → Activate`, with example features under each stage.
- Journey flow: `Customer buys on Shopee → claims points → joins tier → gets AI offer`.
- System diagram: `Purchase channel → Loyalty engine → Customer profile → AMP workflow → LINE message`.
- Opening slide: one market shift, one category anchor, one proof point.

The rule:

> Do not mix levels in a flat list. You may mix levels in a hierarchy, journey, matrix, or system diagram when the layout makes the relationship obvious.

### 10. Use Matrices To Prove Scope

When claiming broad coverage, show the complete map instead of saying "comprehensive."

Example: omnichannel point collection

| | Brand-owned | Third-party |
|---|---|---|
| Online | Website / Shopify | Shopee / Lazada / TikTok Shop |
| Offline | POS | Receipt upload / QR scan |

This proves the scope in one glance.

### 11. Avoid Fake Balance

MECE does not mean every section needs the same number of bullets or slides.

If AI activation needs three slides and rewards needs one, that is fine. Let the system's real complexity determine the space.

Do not pad simple topics for symmetry.

## Feature Communication

### 12. Pass The 5-Second Feature Test

Every feature slide should answer two questions immediately:

1. What feature is this?
2. Why would a marketer, operator, or finance stakeholder care?

If the buyer has to decode a clever phrase before knowing the feature, the slide fails. Prefer plain feature names plus specific copy over abstract benefit language.

Weak:

> Tiers turn progress into a reason to return.

Stronger:

> Tiers & Benefits

Supporting copy:

> Set tier rules by points, spend, orders, or tickets. Rocket evaluates the highest eligible tier first, so high performers can skip levels automatically.

### 13. Use Mental Validation For Feature Scope

When a slide claims a feature has broad scope, prove it with recognizable components at the same abstraction level. Buyers scan feature slides like a checklist.

Weak:

> Flexible reward configuration.

Strong:

> Eligibility, points price, stock, limits, expiry, promo-code pools, and fulfillment are configured on the reward record.

Rules:

- Keep peer items at the same level.
- Keep grammar consistent.
- Use a qualifier only when the next line proves it with examples.
- Use checklist detail only when the slide's job is feature-scope validation. If the slide's job is to explain a concept or operating model, use higher-level parts instead of long feature inventories.
- Put the concept before the inventory. Data fields, feature objects, and product terms are proof examples, not the main message.

Weak:

> See member tier, points, purchases, redemptions, form answers, and support tickets.

Stronger:

> View all customer data in one place, then use the AI customer brief to understand the customer and push the optimal next action instantly. Examples include purchases, points, redemptions, and support history.

### 14. Preserve Product Vocabulary, Explain It For Marketers

CRM Knowledge MCP is the source of truth for feature vocabulary. Do not replace product concepts with looser synonyms if the original term is the product model. Keep the term, then explain it in language a marketer, operator, or CRM owner would understand.

Good hierarchy:

> MCP feature vocabulary → feature model → marketer-understandable explanation → visual support

Bad:

> Missions are challenges with rewards.

Better:

> Missions let marketers set goals for member actions such as purchases, referrals, forms, check-ins, or points earned. Each goal can have outcomes such as points, tickets, rewards, or milestone progress.

Do not rename concepts:

- `Missions`, `goals`, `outcomes`, `progress`, `claim`, `limits`, and `milestones` should not become generic `challenges` and `rewards`.
- `Display Blocks` should not become `app blocks`.
- `Earn Factors` should not become generic `bonus rules` unless used only as supporting explanation.
- `AI Decisioning` should preserve `Act / Wait / Skip`.

Avoid backend/internal terms unless the buyer needs them: `BFF`, `RPC`, `UUID`, `cache`, table names, function names, source IDs, and implementation object names. Keep technical precision in the concept, not in the implementation detail.

Weak:

> Target blocks by persona or guest status, link them to rewards, missions, tiers, or personas, then publish through cached app reads.

Stronger:

> Display Blocks let marketers choose which sections appear on the member home screen, such as profile cards, banners, rewards, missions, tier progress, and quick actions. Blocks can be shown to all members or only to selected personas.

### 15. Show Motion, Not Labels

Abstract labels are hard to picture. Show what happens.

Weak:

> Unified customer profile across all channels.

Strong:

> A customer buys from Shopify, Shopee, POS, or distributor. Rocket records the purchase, calculates points, and adds the activity to one customer profile.

### 16. Describe Features Through Operational Utility

Describe the task the user can perform, not the abstract module name. Use verbs from the buyer's daily work: set rules, launch campaigns, review redemptions, compare segments, approve claims, assign rewards, inspect outcomes.

Weak:

> Campaign management.

Strong:

> Create a mission from purchases, form submissions, referrals, or check-ins; Rocket tracks progress and reward claims automatically.

### 17. Describe AI By The Decision It Makes

Do not sell AI as a label. Name the decision or action it performs.

Weak:

> AI-powered personalization.

Strong:

> AI detects churn risk, chooses the right offer, and sends it before the customer disappears.

Rocket AI should be framed as activation:

> Analyze → Decide → Act

Not:

> Analyze → Dashboard → Human still does the work

### 18. Proof Comes Before Deep Feature Detail

Before showing many features, give the buyer a reason to trust the system.

Use:

- Relevant customer logo
- One metric
- Before / after snapshot
- Concrete implementation result
- Product screenshot that proves the claim

Avoid generic logo dumping if it does not support the buyer's decision.

### 19. Features Must Map To Buyer Pain

Every feature slide should answer:

1. What marketing problem does this solve?
2. What action does the team take?
3. What does Rocket automate or make visible?
4. What proof or example makes it credible?

Example format:

> Problem → Rocket behavior → Marketing outcome → Proof

For compact feature slides, use this simpler structure:

> Feature name → concrete configuration scope → what happens in the member/admin experience → why the team cares

## Sales Persuasion

### 20. Name The Cost Of Doing Nothing

Marketing teams need urgency, not just product understanding.

Useful cost-of-inaction angles:

- Customers buy once and disappear.
- Members exist but are inactive.
- Campaigns depend on blanket promotions.
- First-party data remains fragmented.
- Competitors personalize while the brand still sends the same offer to everyone.

### 21. Handle Objections Inside The Deck

Do not wait for objections to appear after the presentation.

Address:

- Setup time
- Integration effort
- Campaign complexity
- Data quality
- Team workload
- Measurement and ROI
- Security / permissions
- Budget and stakeholder approval

Use specific answers, not reassurance.

Weak:

> Easy implementation.

Strong:

> Launch path: demo → configure earn rules and rewards → connect LINE / purchase channels → launch first campaign in 2 weeks.

### 22. Make The Deck Champion-Forwardable

The deck should still make sense when the marketing champion forwards it to CEO, finance, IT, or procurement.

Rules:

- No presenter-only references like "as mentioned."
- Headlines carry the message.
- Acronyms are defined once.
- Proof points are clear without verbal explanation.
- Implementation and next steps are concrete.

## Visual And Slide Craft

### 23. Use Diagrams For System Understanding

Use diagrams when the slide explains:

- Journey
- Workflow
- Integration
- Data movement
- Before / after process
- Role handoff

Do not use diagrams as decoration. The diagram must make the system easier to understand than text would.

Do not describe the diagram unless it needs interpretation. Good visual-heavy slides use text to state the implication, decision, or model behind the visual:

- Timeline visual: headline names the journey or system loop.
- Architecture visual: headline names the operating model.
- Product screenshot: headline names the buyer task or proof point.
- Diagram labels: short nouns or verbs only; the body carries the meaning.

### 24. Keep Text Short, But Not Vague

Short is good only if it remains specific.

Apply the one-breath test to feature headlines. If the headline cannot be read in one short breath, it is usually trying to do the work that belongs in the body copy.

Weak:

> Flexible campaigns.

Strong:

> Create missions, referrals, check-ins, and tier challenges without developer work.

### 25. Put Technical Detail In Appendix Unless It Supports The Sales Point

Main deck:

- Buyer problem
- System behavior
- Outcome
- Proof
- Plan

Appendix:

- API details
- Field names
- Admin settings
- Security architecture
- Long feature inventory
- Full implementation notes

## Closing

### 26. End With A Plan, Not "Questions?"

The closing slide should reduce uncertainty.

Use a simple plan:

> 1. Demo loyalty workflows  
> 2. Configure member, points, and rewards logic  
> 3. Launch first campaign in 2 weeks

### 27. Make The CTA Specific

Weak:

> Let us know if interested.

Strong:

> Book a 45-minute Loyalty CRM workflow demo with your marketing and CRM team.

Best CTA format:

> Action + audience + timing / outcome

## Quality Checklist

- Does the cover pass the 5-second test: what is it, and who is it for?
- Is the deck written from the buyer's point of view?
- Does the structure follow a clear decision story?
- Does each slide have one conclusion?
- Does the copy compound with the visual instead of describing it?
- Do feature headlines make the product category clear? If the benefit headline is vague, use the feature name.
- Can someone understand the argument from slide titles alone?
- Do feature slides pass the 5-second test: what feature is this, and why should this stakeholder care?
- Do broad feature claims include concrete checklist items at the same abstraction level?
- Do Thai slides keep market-standard technical terms in English when Thai translation would be awkward or less clear?
- Are peer lists at the same abstraction level?
- Are overview and journey slides using hierarchy or flow, not random mixed lists?
- Is the core Rocket story clear: Convert → Engage → Activate?
- Are AI claims described through specific decisions and actions?
- Is proof placed before heavy feature detail?
- Are objections handled directly?
- Is the next step specific enough to put on a calendar?
